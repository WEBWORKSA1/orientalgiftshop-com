Why some gifts are taboo

Most gift taboos in East Asia come from sound-alike words. In Mandarin, "giving a clock" sounds like "attending someone's funeral", "pear" sounds like "separation" and "umbrella" like "breaking apart". The number four is avoided in Chinese, Japanese and Korean because it sounds like the word for death. Other taboos come from symbolism: sharp objects "cut" a relationship, white and black are mourning colours, and in Korea shoes given to a partner are said to make them walk away.

In South Asia, taboos are more often about religion and diet: leather and beef are sensitive in many Hindu and Jain homes, and alcohol or pork are inappropriate for Muslim families anywhere in Asia.

Is it really a big deal to give a clock?

For many younger or internationally raised Chinese people, no. For elders and traditional families, it can genuinely upset people, especially around Lunar New Year or a birthday. Since there are so many good alternatives, it is an easy risk to avoid.

What if I already gave a "taboo" gift?

Don't panic. A common, light-hearted fix in Chinese culture is for the recipient to give you a small coin, turning the gift into a "purchase". Most people appreciate the thought behind a sincere gift.
