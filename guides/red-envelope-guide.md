In much of Asia, cash is not an impersonal gift. Presented in the right envelope, in the right amount and with the right words, it is the most traditional gift of all. ## Chinese red envelopes (hongbao 红包 / lai see 利是)

**When:** Lunar New Year above all, plus weddings, birthdays, a baby's first month, graduations and business openings.

**Who gives to whom:** At New Year, married couples and elders give to children and unmarried younger relatives. Working adults often give to their parents and grandparents as thanks. Employers give to staff, and in Hong Kong many people give small envelopes to doormen, cleaners and service staff they see regularly.

**Amounts:** Use **even numbers** and avoid any 4. Popular choices include 6 (smooth), 8 (prosperity), 88, 168 ("prosperity all the way") and 888. For weddings, guests traditionally aim to cover at least the cost of their seat at the banquet; close family give more.

**Etiquette:**
- Use **crisp new notes**. Banks see queues for new notes before New Year.
- **Don't use coins** (except as a token) and don't mix many small notes.
- Hand over with **both hands** and a greeting. Receive with both hands and say thank you.
- **Don't open it in front of the giver.**
- In Hong Kong and southern China, lai see amounts for casual acquaintances are often small; it is the gesture that counts.

## Vietnamese lì xì

At Tết, **lì xì** envelopes in red or gold carry new notes to children and elders. The amount matters less than the freshness of the notes and the wishes spoken. Avoid amounts with 4; 8 and 9 are popular.

## Japanese otoshidama and goshugi

**Otoshidama (お年玉)** is New Year money for children, given in small, colourful *pochibukuro* envelopes. Amounts usually scale with the child's age.

**Goshugi (ご祝儀)** is wedding money in a decorative *shūgi-bukuro* with a mizuhiki cord tied in a knot that cannot be undone, symbolising a once-in-a-lifetime event. The usual amount from a friend or colleague is **30,000 yen**; family give more. **Odd leading digits** (10,000, 30,000, 50,000) are preferred. **Never 4 or 9.** Always use brand-new notes, facing the same way. For funerals (*kōden*), use slightly used notes, because new notes would suggest you had prepared for the death.

## Korean wedding and New Year money

**Sebaetdon (세뱃돈)** is New Year money given to children after they bow to elders at Seollal. **Chukuigeum (축의금)**, wedding money, goes in a **white envelope** marked 축 결혼, and is handed in at the reception desk. Odd units are customary: 50,000 won for acquaintances and 100,000 won or more for close friends.

## Indian shagun

Indian cash gifts (**shagun**) add one to a round number: **101, 251, 501, 1,001, 2,001** (larger sums are also given as 1,100, 2,100, 5,100 or 11,000). The extra one means the blessing is not "complete", it keeps growing. Shagun envelopes are often decorated and may have a coin attached. They are given at weddings, Diwali, birthdays, a child's naming ceremony and when meeting a newborn.

## Digital red envelopes

Digital red envelopes on apps such as WeChat and Alipay are now a huge part of Chinese New Year, especially between friends and colleagues. Group "lucky draw" envelopes, where a total is split randomly among members, are a popular game. For elders and formal occasions, paper envelopes remain more respectful.

## Red envelopes in business

Many companies give staff red envelopes at the first working day after Lunar New Year (*kai gong* or *hoi nin*). If you manage a team in Asia, ask local colleagues about customary amounts.
