In many Asian markets, gifts are a normal part of building business relationships. Done well, they show respect and long-term commitment. Done badly, they can embarrass a partner or breach compliance rules. This guide covers what experienced teams do.

## Know the gifting calendar

| Market | Main business gifting moments |
|---|---|
| China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia | Lunar New Year (Feb 6, 2027), Mid-Autumn (Sep 15, 2027) |
| Japan | Ochūgen (early July), Oseibo (December 1–20) |
| South Korea | Seollal (Feb 6, 2027), Chuseok (Sep 15, 2027) |
| India | Diwali (Oct 29, 2027) |
| Vietnam | Tết (Feb 6, 2027), Mid-Autumn |
| Indonesia, Malaysia | Eid al-Fitr (around March 10, 2027) |

Plan at least **eight weeks ahead** for branded gifts. Supply chains slow sharply before Lunar New Year and Diwali.

## Choosing the right gift

**Consumables win.** Premium tea, mooncakes, dry fruits, chocolates, fruit sets, hampers and quality liquor (where appropriate) are welcome, easy to share in an office and leave no clutter.

**Quality over logos.** A well-made product with discreet branding on the packaging or card is more respectful than heavily logoed merchandise.

**Match the hierarchy.** In Japan, Korea and China, the most senior recipient should get the most valuable gift. Never give a junior person a better gift than their boss.

**Share-friendly formats.** For teams, choose individually wrapped items that can be distributed.

**Check dietary and religious rules.** Halal for Muslim recipients in Malaysia, Indonesia and elsewhere; vegetarian and alcohol-free for many Indian recipients.

## Presentation

- Wrap in culturally appropriate colours: red and gold for Chinese markets, bright colours for Korea, saffron and gold for Diwali. Avoid plain white or black.
- In Japan, ask the seller for the correct **noshi** wrapping for the occasion.
- Include a **handwritten card**; a greeting in the recipient's language is a strong signal. See our [Greetings Phrasebook](greetings.html).
- Avoid quantities of four in any pack.

## Compliance

Many organisations and governments limit gifts. Before sending:

- Check your own company's gift policy and the recipient's.
- Be especially careful with **public officials, state-owned enterprises, doctors, journalists and teachers**. Anti-bribery laws such as the US FCPA, the UK Bribery Act, China's anti-corruption rules and Korea's Improper Solicitation and Graft Act apply.
- Keep gifts modest, transparent and recorded; never give cash to business partners.
- Avoid gifting during an active tender or negotiation.

## Common mistakes

1. Sending clocks or watches to Chinese partners.
2. Sending a set of four of anything to Chinese, Japanese or Korean clients.
3. Alcohol or leather gifts to Indian or Muslim recipients.
4. Arriving late: gifts that miss Oseibo's window or arrive after Lunar New Year.
5. Identical gifts for everyone regardless of seniority.
6. Shipping perishable gifts without accounting for customs delays.

## Employee gifts

For teams in Asia, festival gifts are often expected. Lunar New Year red envelopes on the first working day, Diwali boxes and bonuses, Chuseok gift sets and Mid-Autumn mooncakes are standard. For multinational teams, let employees choose from a curated catalogue so each person gets something appropriate.
