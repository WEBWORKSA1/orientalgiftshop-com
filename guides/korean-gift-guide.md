In Korea, gifts express *jeong* (정), the deep sense of affection and connection between people. Gifts are given generously at the two big holidays, at life events and in business, and they come with a clear set of customs that are easy to learn.

## Chuseok and Seollal gift sets

Korea's two major holidays are **Chuseok** (the autumn harvest festival, September 15, 2027) and **Seollal** (Lunar New Year, February 6, 2027). In the weeks before each, supermarkets and department stores fill with **gift sets (선물세트)**: boxed, beautifully packaged collections sent to family, bosses, clients and anyone you want to thank.

| Gift set | Who it suits | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hanwoo (Korean beef) | In-laws, key clients, senior managers | The premium choice; cold-chain delivery |
| Fruit (large apples, Korean pears, persimmons, shine muscat grapes) | Parents, relatives, hosts | Perfect, oversized fruit is the point |
| Red ginseng (홍삼) | Elders, bosses, clients | The prestige health gift |
| Health supplements, honey | Parents, elders | Shows care for wellbeing |
| Spam, cooking oil, tuna sets | Colleagues, staff, relatives | Affordable, practical and genuinely popular |
| Traditional sweets (hangwa, yakgwa) | Hosts, colleagues | Elegant and traditional |
| Premium shampoo, toiletry sets | Staff, colleagues | Practical everyday gift |

Note that **pears are lucky in Korea**, unlike in Chinese culture, where they suggest separation. If your recipient is Korean-Chinese, apples are the safe overlap.

## Money gifts

**New Year money (세뱃돈, sebaetdon).** At Seollal, children perform a deep bow (*sebae*) to elders and receive cash, often in decorative envelopes. Amounts rise with age.

**Wedding money (축의금, chukuigeum).** Guests give cash in a **white envelope** with a greeting such as 축 결혼 ("congratulations on your wedding") written on the front and your name on the back. Hand it in at the reception desk, where it is recorded. Odd numbers of units are customary: 50,000 won is common for acquaintances and colleagues, 100,000 won or more for close friends or if you attend a banquet at an upscale venue. Avoid amounts containing 4. Use new notes if you can. Our [Lucky Amount Calculator](lucky-amount.html) helps.

**Condolence money (부의금).** Also in a white envelope, marked 부의 or 근조, with odd units.

## Taboos to know

- **Red ink for names.** Never write a living person's name in red; it is used for the names of the deceased.
- **Shoes for a partner.** Said to make them "run away". Some couples get around it by the recipient paying a token coin.
- **Handkerchiefs.** Associated with tears and sorrow.
- **The number 4 (사, sa),** which sounds like the Sino-Korean word for death. Many buildings label the fourth floor "F".
- **Knives and scissors,** which suggest cutting a relationship.
- **Yellow or white chrysanthemums,** which are funeral flowers.

## How to give and receive

- Offer and receive gifts with **both hands**, or with the right hand while the left hand supports the right forearm, especially with elders.
- A recipient may **decline politely** at first; offer again.
- Gifts are usually **opened later.**
- **Reciprocity** matters: if you receive a gift, a return gesture of similar value is appreciated.
- Wrapping in bright colours such as red, yellow or pink is cheerful and appropriate; avoid plain white or black wrapping for happy occasions.

## Business gifts in Korea

Gifts are common in Korean business culture, especially at the first meeting and before the holidays. Give quality items from your home country or premium Korean gift sets, present the most senior person's gift first and make sure senior gifts are higher in value. Be aware of Korea's **Improper Solicitation and Graft Act** (the "Kim Young-ran Act"), which caps the value of gifts to public officials, journalists and teachers; check the current limits before gifting to anyone in those groups. ## Everyday occasions

- **Housewarming (집들이):** toilet paper and laundry detergent are traditional, jokingly wishing that life "unrolls smoothly" and that wealth "bubbles up" like suds.
- **Birthdays:** seaweed soup is eaten on birthdays; gifts are similar to Western norms.
- **Pepero Day (November 11):** friends and couples exchange stick-shaped chocolate snacks.
- **Visiting a home:** bring fruit, a drink or a dessert, never empty-handed.
