Gift giving in Japan is frequent, thoughtful and shaped by a strong sense of reciprocity. Gifts are less about surprise and more about maintaining relationships: thanking someone, apologising, marking the season, or bringing something back from a trip. The good news is that the rules are consistent, and visitors who make a sincere effort are always appreciated.

## The core principles

- **Presentation matters as much as the gift.** Neat wrapping, often by the shop itself, is expected. Japanese department stores wrap beautifully for free.
- **Consumables are ideal.** Sweets, tea, fruit, coffee and good-quality food do not create clutter or obligation.
- **Modesty in giving.** People often say *tsumaranai mono desu ga* ("it's a trifling thing, but…") when handing over even a generous gift.
- **Give and receive with both hands,** with a slight bow.
- **Gifts are usually opened later,** not in front of the giver, unless the giver invites it.
- **Reciprocity.** A gift often prompts a return gift (*okaeshi*), commonly worth about a third to a half of the original for events such as weddings or funerals.

## The main gift occasions

**Omiyage (お土産).** After any trip, even a short business trip, it is customary to bring back regional snacks for colleagues, friends and family. Choose a box of individually wrapped sweets with enough pieces for everyone on the team. Visitors to Japan often bring omiyage from their home country, which is a lovely gesture for hosts.

**Temiyage (手土産).** A small gift you bring when visiting someone's home or office: sweets, fruit or a bottle. Hand it over after greetings, not at the door.

**Ochūgen (お中元).** The summer gift season, roughly the first half of July in the Tokyo area (into mid-August in some regions). People send gifts to those they are indebted to: bosses, clients, teachers, doctors. Chilled consumables are popular: premium fruit, somen noodles, beer, juice, coffee.

**Oseibo (お歳暮).** The year-end gift season, from early December to about December 20. A thank-you for the year's support, often food for the New Year table: ham, seafood, sake, seasonings. If you miss the window, send the gift in early January labelled *onenga* (New Year gift), or later as *kanchū mimai* (mid-winter greeting).

**Otoshidama (お年玉).** New Year money for children, given in small decorated envelopes called *pochibukuro*. Amounts typically rise with the child's age.

**Weddings.** Guests give cash (*goshugi*), commonly 30,000 yen from a friend or colleague, in brand-new notes inside a decorative envelope (*shūgi-bukuro*). Odd leading digits are preferred because they cannot be divided evenly. Avoid 4 and 9. The couple gives return gifts (*hikidemono*) to guests.

**Valentine's Day and White Day.** In Japan, women traditionally give chocolate on February 14: *honmei choco* to a partner, *giri choco* ("obligation chocolate") to colleagues. Men return the favour on White Day, March 14.

## Numbers and colours

- **Avoid 4** (*shi*, which sounds like death) and **9** (*ku*, which sounds like suffering). Never give a set of four cups or plates.
- **Odd numbers** (3, 5, 7) are generally auspicious; **8** is lucky because the character 八 widens towards the bottom, suggesting growth.
- **Red and white** together are celebratory. **Black and white**, or plain white wrapping with black, signals a funeral.

## Items to avoid

- **Combs** (*kushi*, sounds like "suffering and death").
- **Potted plants for hospital visits:** roots suggest the illness will take hold. Bring cut flowers (not white) or fruit.
- **White flowers, chrysanthemums and lilies,** which are associated with funerals.
- **Knives or scissors for weddings,** as they suggest cutting ties.
- **Stationery or watches for a superior,** which can imply they should work harder or watch the time.
- **Handkerchiefs** can suggest farewell (*tegire*, a cut of ties), though they are a common return gift in some contexts.

## Noshi: the formal wrapping

For formal gifts, shops attach a *noshi-gami*, a paper band printed with a decorative cord (*mizuhiki*), the purpose of the gift (for example お中元 or 御礼, "thanks") and the giver's name. The knot style matters:

| Mizuhiki style | Use for | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Chō-musubi (bow knot) | Births, festivals, thanks, Ochūgen, Oseibo | Easily retied: may it happen again |
| Musubi-kiri (tight knot) | Weddings, get-well, condolences | Cannot be untied: once only |

When you buy a gift in Japan, simply tell the shop the occasion and they will choose the correct noshi.

## Business gifts

Exchange gifts at the end of a first meeting rather than the start. Give to the most senior person first, or give a group gift to the company. A quality product from your home country, beautifully wrapped, is ideal; avoid your own company's logo merchandise as the main gift.
