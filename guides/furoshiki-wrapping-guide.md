A **furoshiki** (風呂敷) is a square of cloth used in Japan for centuries to wrap and carry things. The name means "bath spread", from the Edo period when people bundled their clothes in a cloth at public bathhouses. Today it is one of the most beautiful and sustainable ways to wrap a gift: no tape, no scissors, no waste, and the cloth becomes part of the present.

## Choosing a cloth

| Size | Good for |
|---|---|
| 45–50 cm | Small boxes, books, jars |
| 68–75 cm | Most gift boxes, a bento box, one or two bottles |
| 90–105 cm | Large boxes, carrying bags, multiple items |

Cotton is easy to tie and grips well. Silk and rayon look luxurious but are slippery; tie them a little tighter. Polyester is cheap, colourful and machine washable. A large silk scarf or tea towel works in a pinch.

**Colours and patterns:** Bright colours and auspicious motifs, such as cranes (longevity), plum blossom (resilience), waves (seigaiha, calm and continuity) or pine and bamboo, suit celebrations. Avoid plain dark grey or black for happy occasions.

## The one knot you need: ma-musubi (square knot)

1. Take two diagonal corners and cross the right over the left.
2. Tie once, as if starting to tie a shoe.
3. Cross the ends again, this time left over right, and tie a second time.
4. Pull the two ends apart. A correct square knot sits flat, with the ends pointing sideways.

To untie it quickly, pull one end straight back over the knot and the whole thing slides open.

## Three wraps to start with

**1. Otsukai-tsutsumi (the basic box wrap).** Lay the cloth as a diamond and place the box in the centre. Fold the bottom corner over the box, then the top corner over that. Bring the two side corners up and tie a square knot on top.

**2. Bin-tsutsumi (bottle wrap).** Stand the bottle in the centre. Tie the top and bottom corners together above the bottle. Wrap the two side corners around the bottle in opposite directions and tie at the front, or twist and knot them into a carrying handle.

**3. Hira-tsutsumi (flat, formal wrap).** For the most formal gifts, fold the cloth over the box from each side in turn without any knot, tucking the last corner neatly. This is the way to wrap cash envelopes and formal gifts.

## When to use furoshiki

- Host gifts and omiyage when visiting a Japanese home
- Wine, sake or oil bottles as a hostess gift
- Zero-waste holiday wrapping
- Wrapping a bento box as a lunch gift
- As a gift in itself, with a smaller present inside

## Etiquette

When you give a gift in furoshiki in a formal setting, Japanese convention is to unwrap it and present the gift itself, then take the cloth home. With friends, giving the cloth too is part of the gift; say so, so the recipient knows to keep it.
